# A Brief Introduction to Solipsism

**Solipsism** is the philosophical view that only one's own mind is certain to exist. From the Latin *solus* ("alone") and *ipse* ("self"), it holds that everything outside the self—other minds, the external world, even one's own body—may be nothing more than a projection or construction of one's own consciousness.

It is less a positive doctrine than a **limit position**: the endpoint of radical skepticism about the external world and other minds.

## Core Claim

At its strongest, solipsism asserts:

* I exist.
* My mental states exist.
* Everything else—other people, objects, the physical universe—is either doubtful or wholly dependent on my mind.

The key point is **epistemic asymmetry**: I have direct, privileged access to my own thoughts and experiences, but only indirect access to anything "outside" them.

## Varieties

* **Metaphysical (ontological) solipsism** – Only the self truly exists; everything else is an illusion or projection. Rarely held seriously, as it is nearly impossible to live by.
* **Epistemological solipsism** – I cannot *know* anything exists beyond my own mind, even if other things might exist. This is the more defensible and widely discussed form.
* **Methodological solipsism** – A stance in philosophy of mind (notably in Husserl and some cognitive science) that treats the individual mind as the starting point for analysis, without denying other minds.
* **Egocentric presentism** – Only my present experience is fully real; other perspectives exist only relative to mine.

## The Classic Problems It Raises

1. **The problem of other minds** – I can observe behavior, but never directly experience another's thoughts. How do I know anyone else is conscious?
2. **The external world** – Dreams, hallucinations, and virtual realities show that experience can seem real without corresponding to an external world. How can I rule out that all experience is like this?
3. **The criterion problem** – If all I ever access are my own mental states, what evidence could possibly count against solipsism? This is what makes it so hard to refute.

## Common Responses

* **Descartes** – Used radical doubt as a method but argued that God's non-deceptive nature guarantees the external world.
* **Wittgenstein** – Argued that the solipsist's language is incoherent: a "private language" referring only to private sensations cannot have meaning, because meaning depends on shared public rules.
* **Pragmatism (e.g., Peirce, Rorty)** – Treats solipsism as unfalsifiable and therefore not worth taking seriously; it makes no practical difference.
* **Phenomenology (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty)** – Grants the starting point of subjective experience but argues that consciousness is always *intentional*—directed toward a world—and intersubjectivity is built into experience itself.
* **Ordinary language philosophy** – Points out that we learn concepts like "mind," "real," and "other" in social contexts; radical solipsism strips these words of their normal use.

## In Short

Solipsism is the view that **only my own mind is real or knowable**. Some view it as the logical extreme of asking *"How do I know that anyone I am talking to is real and not my imagination?"*

Most philosophers treat it as **unrefutable but unlivable**—a boundary marker that clarifies the assumptions behind knowledge, reality, and our relations to others. Its value may lie less in being true than in forcing us to examine *why* we believe other minds and a shared world exist at all.
