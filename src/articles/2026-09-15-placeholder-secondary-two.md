# A Brief Introduction to Ontology

**Ontology** is the branch of philosophy concerned with **being**, **existence**, and **reality**. From the Greek *ontos* ("being") and *logos* ("study"), it asks the most basic question of all: **What is there?** and **What does it mean for something to exist?**

## Core Questions

* What kinds of things exist?
* What is the nature of existence itself?
* Are there fundamental categories of being (objects, properties, relations, events, processes)?
* What is the difference between what *appears* to exist and what *really* exists?
* Do abstract entities (numbers, concepts, meanings) exist, and if so, in what sense?

## Major Positions

* **Realism** – There is a mind-independent reality that exists regardless of whether anyone perceives or thinks about it.
* **Idealism** – Reality is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent; what we call "the world" is inseparable from consciousness or thought.
* **Materialism / Physicalism** – Everything that exists is ultimately physical matter or energy.
* **Dualism** – Reality comprises two fundamentally different kinds of substance (e.g., mind and body).
* **Nominalism vs. Realism about universals** – Do general categories (like "redness" or "humanity") exist independently, or are they just names we apply to particular things?
* **Process ontology** – Reality is fundamentally made of processes and change rather than static substances (associated with Alfred North Whitehead and, in anthropology, Tim Ingold).

## Ontology vs. Epistemology

These two are often paired but are distinct:

A, perhaps, useful shorthand: **ontology is about the world; epistemology is about our access to it.**

## Ontology in Anthropology

Ontology has become increasingly important in anthropology, especially through the **"ontological turn"** associated with scholars like Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, Philippe Descola, and Tim Ingold.

Key ideas:

* **Multiple ontologies** – Different cultures may not simply *interpret* the same world differently; they may inhabit genuinely different worlds with different kinds of beings, relations, and realities.
* **Challenge to nature/culture divide** – Many societies do not separate "nature" from "culture" the way Western thought typically does. For example, Amazonian perspectivism holds that humans and animals share a common spiritual essence but see different bodily forms.
* **Beyond representation** – Rather than asking how accurately a culture *represents* reality, anthropology asks what realities are *enacted* through practices, rituals, and relationships.
* **Methodological implications** – The anthropologist cannot assume a single universal reality against which other worldviews are measured; instead, different ontologies must be taken seriously on their own terms.

## In Short

Ontology is the study of **what exists and what it means to exist**. Once confined to *metaphysics*, it now plays a significant role in anthropology by forcing the discipline to question whether "reality" itself is singular and universal—or plural and culturally enacted. Where epistemology asks *how we know*, ontology asks *what there is to know*—and whether different peoples might live in genuinely different worlds.
